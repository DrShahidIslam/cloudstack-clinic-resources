This is the flagship shelf of the resources library: a set of short guides to the treatments patients ask about most, written the way we explain them in the clinic. Read the summary first; follow the links only for the treatments that concern you.

Guide to root canal treatment

Inside every tooth is a soft core of nerve and blood vessels called the pulp. When deep decay or a crack lets bacteria reach it, the pulp becomes inflamed or infected, and the tooth aches, often sharply, sometimes at night. Root canal treatment removes the infected pulp through a small opening, cleans and shapes the narrow canals inside the roots, disinfects them and seals them. The tooth stays in your mouth, relieved of pain and protected, usually afterwards with a crown. The guide's key point is simple: the treatment does not weaken a tooth on purpose, it rescues a tooth that would otherwise be removed. Our lead clinician, Dr Shahid Islam, is an endodontist, which means root canal treatment is his specialist field.

Read further: the library's comparison of root canal against extraction and implant, the root canal aftercare notes, the Knowledge Centre's step-by-step root canal guide, and the clinic's root canal treatment page.

Guide to dental implants

An implant replaces a missing tooth from the root up. A small titanium post is placed in the jawbone, where it fuses with the bone over the following months. Once firm, it carries an abutment and a crown made to match your other teeth. Implants stand on their own, so the neighbouring teeth are not cut down, as they are for a bridge, and they pass chewing force into the bone, which helps the bone stay. The honest limits: implants need enough healthy bone, or grafting to build it; they need surgery and patience, because the stages cannot be rushed; and they are not suitable for every patient or every gap. For several missing teeth in a row, fixed options such as All-on-4 use a small number of implants to carry a full arch.

Read further: the dentures against implants comparison, the clinic's dental implants page, its All-on-4 implants page, and the implant cost guide.

Guide to braces and clear aligners

Orthodontic treatment moves teeth into better positions with light, continuous pressure. Fixed braces do this with brackets and a wire, adjusted at regular visits. Clear aligners do it with a series of removable transparent trays, changed every week or two. Both improve far more than looks: crowded teeth are harder to clean, and a poor bite wears teeth unevenly. The summary every patient should remember is that braces ask little of you day to day but show on your teeth, while aligners are discreet but only work for the hours you actually wear them, around 22 hours a day. Treatment length depends on how far the teeth need to move, and a retainer afterwards holds the result.

Read further: the library's braces against aligners comparison, the Knowledge Centre's braces care guide, and the clinic's pages on orthodontics and invisible aligners.

Guide to teeth whitening

Teeth darken with tea, coffee, tobacco and age. Professional whitening uses a controlled bleaching gel, applied at the clinic or in fitted trays at home, to lift stains from within the enamel rather than scrubbing them off the surface. It works on natural teeth; it does not change the colour of crowns, veneers or fillings, which sometimes need replacing afterwards to match. Sensitivity during treatment is common and usually settles. A cleaning first often improves the result, because whitening gel cannot work evenly through a layer of tartar. Whitening is a cosmetic treatment, so the guide's honest summary is that it brightens healthy teeth; it does not repair them.

Read further: the clinic's teeth whitening page, its whitening cost guide, and the whitening aftercare notes in this library.

Guide to dentures

Dentures are removable replacements for missing teeth, made to fit your gums and restore eating, speech and the shape of your face. A partial denture fills some gaps and clips around the teeth that remain; a complete denture replaces a full arch. Modern dentures are lighter and more natural in appearance than the ones many patients remember, but they still ask for adaptation: the first weeks bring extra saliva, sore spots that need adjustment, and a learning curve with harder foods. Because the jawbone slowly changes shape under a denture, fit loosens over the years and relines or replacement become necessary. Good daily care, set out in our dedicated guide, keeps a denture comfortable and fresh for longer.

Read further: the library's dentures care guide, the dentures against implants comparison, and the clinic's dentures page.

Guide to children's dental visits

Children's dentistry works best when the first visits are easy ones: a look, a count of the teeth, a polish, and home before anything can become a bad memory. Baby teeth matter more than many parents realise; they hold space for the adult teeth, and decay in them causes real pain and missed school. The guide covers what a children's appointment involves, how fluoride and fissure protection help, and how parents can prepare a child: positive words, no promises that nothing will hurt, and no dental horror stories from adults. At this clinic, children's appointments are part of family dentistry, and parents can ask for Dr Vinessa Gill, our female dentist, if their child is more comfortable that way.

Read further: the clinic's paediatric dentistry page and its page about the clinic's female dentist in Karachi, plus the library's first visit checklist, which works for a child's first visit too.

How these guides are written

Every guide in this library follows the same rules. Plain language first, technical terms explained when they appear. No prices, because the clinic quotes after examination, as our cost explainer sets out. No promises of results, because honest dentistry does not come with guarantees. And the same clinic facts everywhere: one address, one phone number, one set of hours, whichever of the clinic's sites you read.
