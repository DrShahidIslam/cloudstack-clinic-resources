Dental X-Ray Guide: Types, Safety and When You Need One

A dentist's eyes, however experienced, can only see the surfaces of your teeth. Most of what decides a treatment plan sits out of sight: decay creeping between two teeth, infection at the tip of a root, the level of the bone holding your teeth, a wisdom tooth lying sideways in the jaw. X-rays are how a dentist sees those things before starting work. This guide explains why X-rays are taken, what the common types show, and how safety is handled, so the request for an image never feels like a surprise.

Why X-rays are taken

An X-ray is a diagnostic tool, not a formality. Dentists take them to find decay in places a probe cannot reach, to see the shape and number of roots before a root canal, to confirm whether infection has spread into the bone, to check the bone before placing an implant, to follow the position of unerupted or impacted teeth, and to watch children's jaws as adult teeth develop. Skipping the image to save a small cost is false economy: treating a tooth blind is how hidden problems turn into failed work and larger bills. It is also why this clinic quotes after examination, as our page on how dental costs are quoted explains. The X-ray is often the piece of evidence the whole plan rests on.

A periapical film is the workhorse of a clinic like ours, where root canal treatment is the speciality: it shows the whole tooth and the bone beyond the root, which is exactly where endodontic problems live. An OPG gives the wide picture when several areas are in question at once.

Safety, in plain words

Dental X-rays use small amounts of radiation, and modern digital systems use less than older film systems, because the sensor needs a shorter exposure. The dose from a single small dental X-ray is comparable to what you receive from natural background radiation over a short period of ordinary life, and far below a medical CT scan of the head. Three habits keep exposure as low as reasonably possible: images are taken only when they will change a decision, not by routine; the beam is aimed narrowly at the area of interest; and protective aprons are used where appropriate, particularly for children. Tell your dentist if you are pregnant or might be. Necessary images can usually still be taken safely with protection, but your dentist may choose to postpone anything that can wait, and that choice is yours to discuss. The risk of missing a hidden infection is, in most real cases, larger than the risk from the image that would have found it.

What happens during an X-ray at the clinic

The process is quick. For a periapical image, a small sensor is positioned inside your mouth against the tooth, you hold still for a few seconds, and the image appears on screen almost at once. The dentist then goes through the image with you, pointing out what is healthy and what is not, because the X-ray is also your evidence: it is the reason behind the treatment plan and its cost. If you have had recent X-rays taken elsewhere, bring them, or a photograph of them, to your first visit; the first visit checklist lists them among the things worth carrying. Good images may not need repeating.
