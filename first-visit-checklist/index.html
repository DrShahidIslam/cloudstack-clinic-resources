Checklist

Dental Checkup Karachi

A first dental visit goes better when you arrive prepared. The dentist can diagnose faster, you remember to ask the questions that matter, and nothing important is left to a guess. This checklist covers what to bring, what will happen during the appointment, and what to make sure of before you leave. It is written for a first visit to this clinic, but most of it applies at any good dental practice.

What to bring

  • Your CNIC or another ID. Clinics keep patient records under your name and contact details; an ID keeps the record accurate from day one.
  • Any previous dental records or X-rays you have. Old X-rays can save repeat imaging and show how a problem has developed over time. Even a photograph of a previous report helps.
  • A list of medicines you take regularly. Include blood thinners, diabetes and blood pressure medicines, and anything you have taken for this tooth, such as antibiotics or painkillers, with doses if you know them.
  • Your allergy history. Note any reaction to medicines, latex, or anaesthetics, even a mild one from years ago.
  • Notes on your symptoms. When the problem started, what triggers it (hot, cold, biting, night-time), and how strong it is. Pain that wakes you at night, or pain from heat that lingers, points the diagnosis in a particular direction, so say it plainly.
  • Your questions. Written down, not left to memory. Section four below lists the ones worth asking.

What happens at a first visit

The visit follows a simple order. First, your history: the dentist asks about the problem, your general health and your medicines. Then the examination itself, tooth by tooth where needed, checking the teeth, gums and any existing fillings or crowns. If the problem sits below the gum or inside a tooth, an X-ray comes next; the dental X-ray guide in this library explains what each type of image shows and why it is needed.

With the examination and images together, the dentist explains the findings in plain words: what is wrong, what will happen if it is left, and what the treatment options are, with the honest advantages and limits of each. You then agree a plan together, and you are told the cost of that plan before treatment begins. At this clinic, that examination-first order is fixed policy, and our page on how costs are quoted explains the reasoning behind it.

Questions to ask any dentist

  • What exactly is wrong with the tooth, and can you show me on the X-ray?
  • What happens if I leave it for now?
  • What are my options, and which do you recommend, and why?
  • How many visits will it take, and how long is each one?
  • What does the full plan cost, and does that figure include everything?
  • What should I expect afterwards: pain, swelling, time off work?
  • How do I care for the tooth or the new work at home?

There are no wrong questions on this list. A dentist who would rather not answer them is giving you useful information about the practice.

Before you leave

Make sure you leave with three things: a clear picture of the diagnosis, a treatment plan you understand, and the agreed next step, whether that is a booked treatment visit, a prescription, or a decision to review the tooth after a short interval. If you have been given aftercare instructions, the aftercare guides in this library cover the common treatments in more depth. Keep any written plan and receipts with your other medical papers; they are part of your dental record.
